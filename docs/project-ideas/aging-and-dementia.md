## Assessment

Age is the strongest known risk factor for dementia, but dementia is not an inevitable result of normal aging. Dementia is a syndrome caused by multiple diseases, with Alzheimer's being the most common cause; Labeling "aging," "memory decline," "Alzheimer's disease," and "dementia" as the same directly affects risk models and product interpretations.[WHO Dementia Special, accessed 2026-09-09](https://www.who.int/health-topics/dementia)

This Hackathon can analyze support gaps and care tasks through public surveys, as well as verify trial registration, research funding, or model evaluations. The value of newly added data lies in clearer questions, fields, and validation methods; The probability of future dementia and actual service decisions are still outside the scope already supported by these archetypes.[Data correspondence with project basis, 2026-09-09](../data-resources/data-resources-extended-eldercare.md)

## Why demand continues to grow

The WHO estimates that in 2021, about 57 million people worldwide had dementia, with nearly 10 million new cases each year; In 2019, the global economic cost was approximately $1.3 trillion. Population, incidence, and cost statistics differ from the same year, so they cannot be multiplied to determine the 2026 market size. Families and other informal caregivers bear a large amount of caregiving work, so risk studies also affect service capacity and household burden.[WHO Facts page, updated on 2026-07-03](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/dementia)

In its 2025 plan statement, the Norwegian government cited that the country had over 100,000 dementia patients in 2020, and noted that demographic changes will increase future demand for services. This is a yearly estimate for countries that cannot directly rank the number of registrations based on different calibers from other countries.[Norwegian Government, 2025-06-16](https://www.regjeringen.no/no/aktuelt/regjeringen-med-ny-demensplan-i-2026/id3109128/)

Demand growth does not mean that any risk tool will be useful. For public health departments, the challenge is how to cover interventionable factors and service gaps; For primary care, the issue is how to identify those who need further evaluation and complete referrals; For families, the question is how to obtain continuous support. The inputs, responsible persons, and success indicators for the three scenarios differ and should be designed separately.

## Aging and Risk: What Does the Existing Evidence Show?

The 2024 Lancet Committee lists 14 potentially modifiable factors: lower education level, hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, insufficient physical activity, depression, traumatic brain injury, social isolation, air pollution, and untreated vision loss. The report estimates that full lifecycle action targeting these factors could prevent or delay about 45% of dementia cases. This is a population estimate based on causal assumptions and the distribution of risk factors, and cannot be interpreted as a 45% reduction in risk for a person after following the checklist, nor can it simply add up the proportions of each factor.[Lancet Commission, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39096926/);[Notes from the lead institution UCL, 2024-07-31](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2024/jul/nearly-half-dementia-cases-could-be-prevented-or-delayed-tackling-14-risk-factors)

The risk factor list fits organizational evidence but is not a ready-made personal scoring formula. It is especially important to distinguish between the age of exposure, timing of measurement, and disease prodromal symptoms: late-life depression or reduced activity may be influenced by changes in the disease that have already occurred. Products should display the study population and chronological order, avoiding interpreting correlations as already established reversible causal relationships.[UCL, 2024-07-31, same as above](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2024/jul/nearly-half-dementia-cases-could-be-prevented-or-delayed-tackling-14-risk-factors)

In July 2026, WHO released the second edition of the Guidelines on Cognitive Decline and Dementia Risk Reduction, adding environmental factors and individualized multifactorial interventions, and the 2019 edition should no longer be labeled as the latest version. The WHO's recommendation system and Lancet's risk attribution model answer different questions, so the difference in the number of factors between the two should not be treated as negating each other.[WHO Second Edition Guideline, 2026-07-15](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240123557)

## Current Policies and Nordic Focus

### Sweden: National Strategy and Collaboration in Diagnosis and Care

In January 2025, the Swedish government released the "Varje dag räknas" national dementia strategy, implemented from 2025 to 2028, focusing on care development and requiring tracking of target progress. The strategy complements the national guidelines of Socialstyrelsen.[Swedish Government, 2025-01-23](https://www.regeringen.se/informationsmaterial/2025/01/varje-dag-raknas-nationell-demensstrategi-20252028/)

Socialstyrelsen's official portal lists care assessments and indicators for 2025, and states that the new guidelines are planned for release in summer 2027. Therefore, it is currently possible to compare policy with evidence, and the planned new guidelines cannot be used as current rules. The most specific opportunity for our team is to examine public materials or project claims to see if suspected symptom assessment, multidisciplinary support, and applicable population boundaries are retained.[Socialstyrelsen, updated on 2026-02-17](https://www.socialstyrelsen.se/kunskapsstod-och-regler/regler-och-riktlinjer/nationella-riktlinjer/riktlinjer-och-utvarderingar/demens/)

### Finland: Research and implementation materials for FINGER

Finland's FINGER randomized trial provides a direct research basis for multifactorial intervention. THL also released FINGER model materials to promote cognitive and functional development in older adults, demonstrating that the research has developed a practical implementation framework.[THL, 2022, accessed 2026-09-09](https://www.julkari.fi/items/28c51e2a-8644-44b9-bda5-26f21a1c06ec)

This type of material is suitable for studying "how interventions are delivered, who cannot participate, and how much support is needed." The test and operational materials themselves cannot prove that Finland is universally covered, nor can they prove that the digital reduced version has the same effect; This round did not verify the actual national coverage rate or unified procurement and reimbursement conditions.

### Denmark: From Planning Goals to Implementation Checks

Denmark's National Dementia Action Plan 2025 set targets for 98 dementia-friendly municipalities, completing more assessments with 80% of patients receiving specific diagnoses, and reducing antipsychotic drug use by 50%. The Department of Health page notes that relevant experiences will be summarized in the first half of 2026, including early identification and medication management.[Danish Health Authority, updated on 2025-11-04](https://www.sst.dk/vidensbase/sygdomme-og-behandling/demens/anbefalinger-og-handlingsplaner-paa-demensomraadet)

These are the project objectives and the work arrangements at the time. No materials sufficient to confirm the achievement of all targets or the subsequent implementation of the national plan were not obtained in this round. A suitable item is evidence verification between policy objectives and public results; if results are missing, it shows "Not Verified."

### Norway: The status of subsequent plans needs confirmation

The Norwegian government announced the formulation of Demensplan 2030 in 2025, aiming to focus on prevention, early diagnosis and treatment, personalized services, technology, and the needs of remote towns. ### Iceland: Historical plans can be verified, but current continuation status has not been verified

Alzheimer Europe documented Iceland's launch of the 2020–2025 National Dementia Action Plan in 2020. ### International Guidelines and the EU Treatment Border

The WHO 2026 Second Edition Guidelines can serve as evidence and version benchmarks for risk reduction materials. The guidelines distinguish recommendation strength and evidence certainty for different interventions; Implementation for specific populations still requires professional judgment and local service conditions.[WHO,2026-07-15](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240123557)

Leqembi received EU marketing authorization on April 15, 2025, for patients with early-stage Alzheimer's disease who meet the specified criteria, including confirmed amyloid pathology and APOE ε4 conditions; EMA requires professional supervision and timely MRI examination, while emphasizing the risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA). It is not a preventive medicine for generally healthy elderly people. EU licensing also cannot directly prove that Nordic countries have reimbursed or routinely provided the items, and this round did not verify country by country.[EMA product page, accessed on 2026-09-09](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/human/EPAR/leqembi)

## Which Solutions Have the Strongest Practical Foundation?

Research or policy-based directions include multifactorial lifestyle interventions, professional assessments and continuous services, family support, and public health actions targeting risk factors. Products should help existing services accomplish specific tasks, such as verifying research outcomes, finding current guidance, recording barriers to participation, or supplementing referral materials. Any promise of "reducing dementia risk" requires evidence of target populations and long-term outcomes.

For the three-person weekend team, investigation, recalculation, and evidence verification can form specific acceptance tasks. Investigate and analyze to check denominators, weights, and deficiencies; Evidence checks examine outcomes, populations, and time windows, avoiding writing cognitive improvement as reduced incidence or omitting active controls. This judgment is based on topic recommendations based on delivery and validation conditions, not on the product requirements that have been validated.

### A1. Analysis of support gaps among self-reported cognitive decline

The question was limited to: Among the selected states and people aged 45 and above who reported worsening memory or confusion, which groups reported needing help but did not receive sufficient assistance, and which had not yet been discussed with healthcare workers? The corresponding plan provides a research basis for supporting service planning. Data came from the BRFSS (1) cognitive decline module, which included subjective changes over the past 12 months, impacts of daily activities, need and access to help, work or social impact, and healthcare discussions.[CDC module description, 2024-08-27; inventory verification on 2026-09-09](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-aging-data/brfss/cognitive-decline.html)

The first edition fixes the 2024 Arizona LLCP24V1 using the corresponding _LCPWTV1; Specific field codes and missing values are confirmed according to this version of the codebook. This mapping is already documented in [CDC States–Modules–Documents–Weighting Table, verified on 2026-09-09](https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/questionnaires/modules/state2024.htm). First, the sample screening process for one state, two supporting indicators, and age groupings are shown, without expanding to the national or Nordic map of the United States.

The minimal demonstration starts from a result card into the original question, denominator, number of valid respondents, weighted ratio, and confidence interval. "Those in need of help who do not receive sufficient help" and "Self-reported decliners who do not discuss it" must use their respective off-topic rules and cannot share the same denominator. If the questionnaire provides frequency grading, it should first be displayed according to the original grading, and the rules for merging categories must be fixed before analysis.

Verification includes code frequency, skipping relationships, module weights, layering/PSU, and at least one independent recalculation. Small shot suppression is performed according to pre-recorded CDC analysis rules; Insufficient sample size or conclusions sensitive to missing treatment indicate "insufficient for comparison." Decreased subjective cognition is not diagnosed dementia, and "not discussing with medical staff" does not mean actual referral failure; This program does not triage or certify that any service can reduce disease incidence.
